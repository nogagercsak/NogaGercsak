I was reading a bit during hibernation

May 2, 2026

hello! i have not been as active as i would have liked to be on my substack for the past few months… but i’m back!

one of my goals for 2026 was to read one book every month, and i am proud to say i have been on track with 4 books read (yay!). so in fun nature, i will leave some impromptu and disheveled reviews. (note: two of these books were read for ap lit but i am STILL counting them bc i genuinely enjoyed them (so, shhhh)).

also feel free to follow my very inactive goodreads where i leave single-sentence reviews.

Frankenstein (1818) by Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley

Starting off with a clear classic, Frankenstein was moving and relevant in ways i didn’t expect. I think almost everyone knows what Frankenstein is about so I won’t be giving an intro. First off, I find it interesting how one of the most iconic moments in Frankenstein (the creation of the monster), was actually insignificant in the book. I was expecting a whole shebang with the “IT’S ALIVE!!!” but that part was slightly anti-climactic lol. Moving on, Frankenstein had me questioning humanity’s role in nature. Did Shelley want to warn us of the dangers in getting our hands dirty with playing God?

To what extent can humans create intelligence, life, etc., before it becomes immoral; before our own selfishness will spread and cause even more pain? Is it even moral to give life? I felt so awful for the monster, especially when he was just trying to befriend the villagers:

“I eagerly longed to discover myself to the cottagers, I ought not to make the attempt until I had first become master of their language; which knowledge might enable me to make them overlook the deformity of my figure”

😭. Was his murder run rationalized? Ehhh, I personally don’t think so. But my god his father treated him terribly. ALSO, victor frankenstein was SO unbearable and whiny at times—I couldn’t do it. Moreover, his whole objectification and divine language he used to describe Elizabeth made me uncomfortable—goes to show what an amazing author Shelley is.

To wrap this up, Shelley wonderfully explores how loss, love, nature and science interact to create such layered characters and stories. I found the monster’s learning journey on what it meant to be human particularly fascinating. I could give Frankenstein so much more praise, but once again, it’s considered a classic for a reason.

The Drunkard’s Walk: How Randomness Rules Our Lives by Leonard Mlodinow

To be totally transparent, I bought this book in April 2025 but didn’t actually pick it up until this January. Reading was unfortunately not a huge priority for me during first semester and this book isn’t exactly a page-turner—but it was still amazing! The Drunkard’s Walk: How Randomness Rules Our Lives dives into the world of statistics, probability, psychology, history, and quite a bit of gambling. I found this book enjoyable as Mlodinow’s writing was able to seamlessly blend humor, math, and history. I recommend this book for people, like me, who are by no means experts in these fields and just want a sweet introduction that focuses on many bits in statistics and probability. You will learn about the Bernoulli family drama, how molecules prompted the “Drunkard’s walk,” experiments that explore psychology, and much more! The book is slow at first, but the more I read, the more I got sucked in.

Here are some of my key take-aways: much of our success and failures are actually attributed to random chance (more than you think), and sometimes we attribute randomness to events that actually followed a clear, yet intuitively hidden, path.

Here is a particular quote I liked:

“We must all draw our own conclusions depending on the circumstances, but with an understanding of how randomness operates at least conclusions may not be naïve.”

Martyr! by Kaveh Akbar

Martyr! is about Cyrus Shams, an Iranian-American (or better put: American-Iranian) poet in his late 20s. At less than one year old, Cyrus’s mother died in a tragic plane crash that was accidentally shot down by US forces after being mistaken as a missile. His father, looking for a fresh start, moved him and baby Cyrus to the middle-of-nowhere midwest america. His father worked on a chicken farm and would drink himself to sleep every single night, slowly killing himself. During Cyrus’s freshman year of college, his father passes away. As a reaction to his parents’ meaningless (his words, not mine!) deaths, Cyrus becomes obsessed with martyrdom—trying to understand what meaningful death is.

Quote I loved:

“It’s easy for people who have sacrificed nothing to rationalize their own ordinariness by calling me lucky. But I sacrificed my entire life; I sold it to the abyss. And the abyss gave me art.”

I will keep my review brief so I do not spoil. Martyr! had me a bit confused at the start—and the writing style was definitely unique. Kaveh Akbar’s poetry background flourishes throughout the book, which is beautiful and simultaneously confusing (at least for me😭). Nevertheless, the last half had me HOOKED. I could not put it down. Although I believe many of the characters (especially Cyrus) were quite unlikable at times, the characters all play valuable roles in the themes and storytelling, which I found very satisfying. The ending was so fascinating and had me on reddit forums to read other people’s interpretation. I finished this book thinking about what life truly means, and what my life truly means. Moreover, I believe Akbar did an excellent job of weaving themes of Iranian culture, LGBTQ+, and addiction. Learning about these different sides to human experiences makes me really love reading, and Martyr! truly did remind me what an amazing thing reading can be. Go read Martyr!!

Their Eyes Were Watching God by Zora Neale Hurston

We will be finishing off with an absolute CLASSIC. I mean this book was INSANELY ahead of its time, and Hurston wrote in such incredible ways. I mean she literally invented the word “monstropolous” in this book (among many other words).

Hurston presents our protagonist, Janie, with such extensive thought and depth that it brings me total awe. Their Eyes Were Watching God is set in early 20th-century Florida. Janie was never allowed to know what true love and independence meant, and throughout the novel we see her journey on achieving such. The entire novel explores themes of romance, relationships, and what it means to embark on life on your own, not molding into what others perceive of you.

My fav thing of the whole entire book is how the entire story Janie tells to her own friend, Pheoby, is a testament to her newfound agency, which i think is SO beautiful and full-circle. Hurston, who was also an anthropologist, writes the dialogue with phonetics of Black American Southern dialect, which was extraordinary for the time it was written. Her writing is characterized by extensive metaphors, scriptural tones (not to moralize but to deliver poetic social commentary), and cultural elements. Her famous opening to the novel tells you everything you need to know about what the book offers:

“Ships at a distance have every man’s wish on board. For some they come in with the tide. For others they sail forever on the horizon, never out of sight, never landing until the Watcher turns his eyes away in resignation, his dreams mocked to death by Time. That is the life of men.

Now, women forget all those things they don’t want to remember and remember everything they don’t want to forget. The dream is the truth. Then they act and do things accordingly.”

Like omg. I’m ngl at first I was confused when I read this but Hurston has you finishing the book with understanding the role of every single metaphor and thematic passage. I also find it insane how nearly every single line in this book can be hyper-analyzed and tells you so much about the story and life as a whole.

There are so many quotes that i loved but here are a few:

“There is a basin in the mind where words float around on thought and thought on sound and sight. Then there's a depth of thought, untouched by words, and deeper still a gulf of formless feelings untouched by thought.”

“They seem to be staring at the dark, but their eyes were watching God.”

“It’s uh known fact, Pheoby, you got tuh go there tuh know there. Yo’ papa and yo’ mama and nobody else can’t tell yuh and show yuh. Two things everybody’s got tuh do fuh theyselves. They got tuh go tuh God, and they got tuh find out about livin’ fuh theyselves.”

thanks for reading! ✨
